Mean Mug Growth Analytics

A full hybrid-cloud analytics stack, built to answer one question: can this business trust its own data enough to spend on it?

Self-directed case study for a simulated 3-location coffee shop chain — from intentionally-corrupted raw data through a tested dbt star schema, Python customer analytics, a DuckDB→BigQuery migration, and a Power BI executive dashboard.

The Situation

Mean Mug Coffee is depicted as a business flying blind — running paid campaigns and loyalty programs with no way to verify true incremental lift versus margin cannibalization, making marketing decisions on gut feel.

The project was scoped around three business questions: where should marketing spend go — proven lift or just discount cost? Which customers are trending toward churn? And can leadership actually trust the underlying transaction data — no duplicates, no voids, no orphaned records?

What I Built

The original plan was a 3-page Power BI dashboard as the end goal. Partway through, I made a deliberate scope pivot: instead of optimizing the dashboard, I doubled down on the analytics-engineering layer underneath it — a cloud warehouse migration, real data-quality testing, and semi-structured event data handling. The dashboard became the last mile, not the destination.

Key Findings

Cohort Retention
64.5% → 76%

Jan 2024 cohort held 66% Month-1 / 64.5% Month-2 retention. Feb 2024 improved to ~76% Month-1 — a ~10% month-over-month gain worth investigating further.

Seasonal Buyer Segment
2.6x order frequency, ~4x lifetime spend

Seasonal-product buyers averaged 3.44 lifetime orders / $38.38 lifetime spend vs. 1.31 orders / $9.79 for baseline buyers — reframing them as a high-value segment rather than one-off holiday shoppers.

Customer LTV
$113.91 average historical LTV

Implies a maximum CAC ceiling of $37.97 to sustain a healthy 3:1 LTV:CAC ratio — a direct, usable input for marketing spend decisions.

Campaign Lift — where the raw numbers didn't hold up

Promo 1 (Spring Launch, 20% off) showed AOV rising from $10.96 to $11.37 and basket size from 2.44 to 2.53 items — a promising raw signal. Formal significance testing (see below) found that no comparison reached statistical significance at α = 0.05. The descriptive pattern is real; it just doesn't hold up as a defensible "true lift" claim. I revised the original spend recommendation accordingly rather than keeping the more impressive-sounding but unsupported conclusion.

Statistical Rigor: The Significance Testing Workflow

This is the part of the project I'd point an interviewer to first — not because the result was dramatic, but because the process was correct.

01

Caught an independence violation before testing

56–80% of orders per group came from repeat customers — testing at the order level would have violated the independence assumption. Aggregated to customer-level observations first.

02

Ran assumption checks before choosing a test

Skewness, Shapiro-Wilk, and Levene's test — Levene's rejected the equal-variance assumption, so Welch's t-test was used instead of Student's t-test.

03

Matched effect size to the variance finding

Used Glass's delta (control-group SD) rather than Cohen's pooled-SD, for consistency with the unequal-variance result from Levene's test.

04

Reported the null result honestly

No comparison reached significance at α = 0.05 — the original "genuine incremental lift" framing was corrected rather than kept.

Built with dbt, BigQuery, Power BI, and Python — the statistical testing workflow (Welch's t-test / Glass's delta implementation) was developed with Claude Code.

Engineering Judgment

Diagnosed and prevented a fact-table grain mismatch that would have deflated conversion metrics from 10% to 3.3% due to a fan-out join. In the Power BI layer, I deliberately left the cohort-retention and campaign-lift tables disconnected rather than joining them — joining pre-aggregated Python output at different grains would have caused the same fan-out error again.

I also turned off subtotals on the retention heatmap, since summing retention percentages is mathematically invalid — a formatting decision driven by a statistical judgment call, not a default left unchanged.
